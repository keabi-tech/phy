    Chapter 1

    Projectile Motion

    1.1 Introduction to projectile motion

    Definition

    Projectile motion is the two-dimensional motion of an object projected into the air and moving under the influence of gravitational acceleration, neglecting air resistance.

    Examples

    • A ball kicked into the air.
    • A stone thrown horizontally from a building.
    • A basketball shot toward a basket.
    • A ball thrown from an elevated platform.

    The object follows a curved path called a parabolic trajectory (for ideal projectile motion).

    1.1.1 Classification of projectile motion

    Projectile motion is commonly divided into two cases according to the direction of initial velocity.

    CaseInitial velocityDescription
    A. Horizontal projectionParallel to the horizontal surfaceVelocity makes no angle with the ground at launch.
    B. Angled projectionMakes an angle with the horizontalVelocity has both horizontal and vertical components.
    Key principle

    Both cases are governed by the same fundamental principle: horizontal and vertical motions are independent.

    1.2 Fundamental principles of projectile motion

    Before deriving projectile equations, we need to understand how motion behaves along the two perpendicular directions.

    1.2.1 Horizontal motion (X-axis)

    When an object moves through the air without air resistance, there is no horizontal force acting on it. According to Newton's second law:

    Newton's 2nd law \[F_x = m\,a_x\]

    Since the horizontal resultant force is zero:

    Horizontal \[0 = m\,a_x \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{a_x = 0}\]

    This means horizontal acceleration is zero, so horizontal velocity remains constant. Using the equation of linear motion, \(v = u + at\):

    Horizontal velocity \[\boxed{v_x = u_x}\]

    Horizontal displacement is:

    Horizontal displacement \[\boxed{x = u_x\,t}\]

    1.2.2 Vertical motion (Y-axis)

    Gravity acts vertically downward throughout projectile motion. Taking upward as positive:

    Vertical acceleration \[\boxed{a_y = -g}\]

    Using the equation \(v = u + at\), we obtain:

    Vertical velocity \[\boxed{v_y = u_y - g\,t}\]

    Vertical displacement is \(y = u_y t + \tfrac{1}{2}a_y t^2\). Substituting \(a_y = -g\):

    Vertical displacement \[\boxed{y = u_y\,t - \tfrac{1}{2}g\,t^2}\]
    Important

    Horizontal acceleration is zero, but vertical acceleration is constant and downward.

    Symbols and SI units
    • \(u_y\): initial vertical velocity (m/s)
    • \(v_y\): final vertical velocity (m/s)
    • \(g\): acceleration due to gravity (m/s²), usually 10 m/s² in problems
    • \(t\): time (s)
    • \(y\): vertical displacement (m)

    1.3 Horizontal projectile motion

    1.3.1 Definition

    Definition

    Horizontal projectile motion occurs when an object is projected horizontally from an elevated position with an initial horizontal velocity.

    Examples:

    • A ball rolling off a table.
    • A stone thrown horizontally from a cliff.
    • Water flowing horizontally from a hole in a tank.

    1.3.2 Characteristics

    • Initial horizontal velocity is nonzero.
    • Initial vertical velocity is zero.
    • Horizontal velocity remains constant.
    • Vertical velocity increases downward due to gravity.
    • The object follows a parabolic path.

    1.3.3 Mathematical derivation of horizontal projectile equations

    Derivation

    Suppose an object is projected horizontally with initial velocity \(u\) from a height \(h\).

    Step 1: Horizontal motion. Since horizontal acceleration is zero, \(a_x = 0\). Using \(x = u_x t + \tfrac{1}{2}a_x t^2\):

    \[\boxed{x = u\,t}\]

    Step 2: Vertical motion. Since the object is projected horizontally, \(u_y = 0\). Taking downward as positive, vertical displacement is \(h = u_y t + \tfrac{1}{2}g t^2\). Substituting \(u_y = 0\):

    \[h = \tfrac{1}{2}g\,t^2\]

    Rearranging, \(2h = g t^2\), so \(t^2 = \dfrac{2h}{g}\). Taking the square root:

    Time of flight \[\boxed{t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}}\]

    Step 3: Horizontal range. We already derived \(x = u\,t\). Substituting the time equation:

    Horizontal range \[\boxed{R = u\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}}\]

    1.3.4 Important formulas

    Physical quantityFormula
    Horizontal velocity\(v_x = u\)
    Vertical velocity\(v_y = g\,t\)
    Horizontal displacement\(x = u\,t\)
    Vertical displacement\(h = \tfrac{1}{2} g\,t^2\)
    Time of flight\(t = \sqrt{2h/g}\)
    Horizontal range\(R = u\sqrt{2h/g}\)
    Resultant final velocity\(v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}\)

    1.3.5 Worked example

    Worked example 1.1

    A ball is projected horizontally from a building 45 m high with a velocity of 20 m/s. Calculate:

    • Time taken to reach the ground.
    • Horizontal range.
    • Vertical velocity just before reaching the ground.
    • Resultant velocity just before impact.

    Given: \(h = 45\ \text{m},\quad u = 20\ \text{m/s},\quad g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2\)

    Required: \(t,\ R,\ v_y,\ v\)

    1. Time of flight. Formula: \(t = \sqrt{2h/g}\).
      Substitution: \(t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2(45)}{10}} = \sqrt{9}\)
      \(t = 3\ \text{s}\)
    2. Horizontal range. Formula: \(R = u\,t\).
      Substitution: \(R = 20(3)\)
      \(R = 60\ \text{m}\)
    3. Final vertical velocity. Formula: \(v_y = g\,t\).
      Substitution: \(v_y = 10(3)\)
      \(v_y = 30\ \text{m/s, downward}\)
    4. Resultant velocity. Horizontal and vertical velocities are perpendicular, so by Pythagoras' theorem: \(v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}\).
      Substitution: \(v = \sqrt{20^2 + 30^2} = \sqrt{400 + 900} = \sqrt{1300}\)
      \(v \approx 36.06\ \text{m/s}\)

    1.4 Angled projectile motion

    1.4.1 Definition

    Definition

    Angled projectile motion occurs when an object is projected into the air with an initial velocity making an angle \(\theta\) with the horizontal surface.

    Examples:

    • A football kicked at an angle.
    • A basketball thrown toward a hoop.
    • A stone thrown upward and forward.

    1.4.2 Characteristics

    • Initial velocity has horizontal and vertical components.
    • Horizontal velocity remains constant.
    • Vertical velocity changes due to gravity.
    • At maximum height, vertical velocity becomes zero.
    • The projectile follows a parabolic trajectory.

    1.4.3 Resolving initial velocity into components

    Consider an object projected with initial velocity \(u\) at an angle \(\theta\). Its initial velocity can be divided into two perpendicular components. Using trigonometry:

    Horizontal component \[\cos\theta = \frac{u_x}{u} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{u_x = u\cos\theta}\]
    Vertical component \[\sin\theta = \frac{u_y}{u} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{u_y = u\sin\theta}\]

    These are the initial horizontal and vertical velocity components.

    1.4.4 Equations of angled projectile motion

    A. Horizontal displacement \[x = u_x t \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{x = u\cos\theta \cdot t}\]
    B. Vertical displacement \[y = u_y t - \tfrac{1}{2}g t^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{y = u\sin\theta \cdot t - \tfrac{1}{2}g t^2}\]
    C. Horizontal velocity \[\boxed{v_x = u\cos\theta}\]
    D. Vertical velocity \[\boxed{v_y = u\sin\theta - g\,t}\]

    1.4.5 Time to reach maximum height

    At maximum height, vertical velocity is zero. The object stops moving upward momentarily before descending. Therefore \(v_y = 0\). Using \(v_y = u\sin\theta - gt\):

    \[0 = u\sin\theta - g\,t \quad\Rightarrow\quad g\,t = u\sin\theta\]
    Time to maximum height \[\boxed{t_{up} = \frac{u\sin\theta}{g}}\]

    1.4.6 Maximum height

    Use the vertical equation \(v_y^2 = u_y^2 + 2a_y y\). At maximum height \(v_y = 0\) and \(a_y = -g\):

    \[0 = u_y^2 - 2g\,H \quad\Rightarrow\quad H = \frac{u_y^2}{2g}\]

    Since \(u_y = u\sin\theta\):

    Maximum height \[\boxed{H = \frac{u^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}}\]

    1.4.7 Total time of flight

    For a projectile that lands at the same height as its launch point, the upward and downward times are equal because the vertical motion is symmetrical. Therefore \(T = 2t_{up}\):

    Total time of flight \[\boxed{T = \frac{2u\sin\theta}{g}}\]

    1.4.8 Horizontal range

    Horizontal range is the total horizontal distance travelled before the projectile returns to its original height. Using \(R = u\cos\theta \cdot T\) and substituting the total flight time:

    \[R = u\cos\theta\left(\frac{2u\sin\theta}{g}\right) = \frac{2u^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g}\]

    Using the identity \(\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\):

    Horizontal range \[\boxed{R = \frac{u^2\sin 2\theta}{g}}\]

    1.4.9 Important conclusions

    Maximum range

    Since the maximum value of \(\sin 2\theta\) is 1:

    \[\sin 2\theta = 1 \Rightarrow 2\theta = 90^\circ \Rightarrow \boxed{\theta = 45^\circ}\]

    Thus, the maximum range occurs at a projection angle of \(45^\circ\), provided launch and landing heights are equal.

    Complementary angles

    Two projection angles are complementary when their sum is \(90^\circ\). For example, \(30^\circ + 60^\circ = 90^\circ\). Since:

    \[\sin(2\times 30^\circ) = \sin 60^\circ \quad\text{and}\quad \sin(2\times 60^\circ) = \sin 120^\circ\]

    Both have the same sine value. Therefore, the two angles produce equal horizontal ranges at the same initial speed.

    1.4.10 Trajectory equation

    The trajectory equation describes the relationship between horizontal position \(x\) and vertical position \(y\), without using time. We have \(x = u\cos\theta\cdot t\), so \(t = \dfrac{x}{u\cos\theta}\). Substituting into \(y = u\sin\theta\cdot t - \tfrac{1}{2}g t^2\):

    \[y = u\sin\theta\left(\frac{x}{u\cos\theta}\right) - \frac{1}{2}g\left(\frac{x}{u\cos\theta}\right)^2\]
    Trajectory equation \[\boxed{y = x\tan\theta - \frac{g\,x^2}{2u^2\cos^2\theta}}\]

    This is a quadratic equation in \(x\), which explains why the ideal projectile follows a parabolic path.

    1.4.11 Worked example

    Worked example 1.2

    A football is kicked with an initial velocity of 30 m/s at an angle of \(30^\circ\) above the horizontal. Calculate:

    • Horizontal velocity.
    • Vertical velocity.
    • Time to maximum height.
    • Maximum height.
    • Total time of flight.
    • Horizontal range.

    Given: \(u = 30\ \text{m/s},\quad \theta = 30^\circ,\quad g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2\)

    Use: \(\sin 30^\circ = 0.5,\quad \cos 30^\circ = 0.866\)

    1. Horizontal velocity. Formula: \(u_x = u\cos\theta\).
      Substitution: \(u_x = 30(0.866)\)
      \(u_x = 25.98\ \text{m/s}\)
    2. Vertical velocity. Formula: \(u_y = u\sin\theta\).
      Substitution: \(u_y = 30(0.5)\)
      \(u_y = 15\ \text{m/s}\)
    3. Time to maximum height. Formula: \(t_{up} = \dfrac{u\sin\theta}{g}\).
      Substitution: \(t_{up} = \dfrac{30(0.5)}{10}\)
      \(t_{up} = 1.5\ \text{s}\)
    4. Maximum height. Formula: \(H = \dfrac{u^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}\).
      Substitution: \(H = \dfrac{30^2(0.5)^2}{2(10)} = \dfrac{900(0.25)}{20}\)
      \(H = 11.25\ \text{m}\)
    5. Total time of flight. Formula: \(T = \dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g}\).
      Substitution: \(T = \dfrac{2(30)(0.5)}{10}\)
      \(T = 3\ \text{s}\)
    6. Horizontal range. Formula: \(R = \dfrac{u^2\sin 2\theta}{g}\).
      Substitution: \(R = \dfrac{30^2\sin 60^\circ}{10} = \dfrac{900(0.866)}{10}\)
      \(R = 77.94\ \text{m}\)

    Chapter 1: Unit summary

    Summary
    • Projectile motion is two-dimensional motion under gravity alone, neglecting air resistance.
    • Horizontal and vertical motions are independent: \(a_x = 0\) and \(a_y = -g\).
    • Horizontal projection: \(u_y = 0\), \(t = \sqrt{2h/g}\), \(R = u\sqrt{2h/g}\).
    • Angled projection: \(u_x = u\cos\theta\), \(u_y = u\sin\theta\).
    • Time to maximum height \(t_{up} = \dfrac{u\sin\theta}{g}\); maximum height \(H = \dfrac{u^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}\).
    • Total time of flight \(T = \dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g}\); range \(R = \dfrac{u^2\sin 2\theta}{g}\).
    • Range is maximum at \(\theta = 45^\circ\); complementary angles give equal ranges.
    • The trajectory is parabolic: \(y = x\tan\theta - \dfrac{gx^2}{2u^2\cos^2\theta}\).

    Chapter 1: Exam-focused points

    Exam focus
    • Always resolve the initial velocity into components before using any equation.
    • Remember that \(a_x = 0\) in every projectile problem; do not add a horizontal acceleration term.
    • At maximum height, \(v_y = 0\). This is the key condition for finding \(t_{up}\) and \(H\).
    • Use \(T = 2t_{up}\) only when launch and landing heights are equal.
    • Range is maximum at \(45^\circ\); be ready to justify why complementary angles give the same range.
    • For the resultant velocity, combine perpendicular components with Pythagoras' theorem: \(v = \sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}\).
    • Write Given, Required, Formula, Substitution, Calculation and Final Answer clearly to earn method marks.
    Self-check: why does a ball dropped from rest and one projected horizontally reach the ground together?

    Vertical motion is independent of horizontal motion. Both have \(u_y = 0\) and the same \(a_y = -g\), so they fall the same height in the same time.

    Self-check: what is \(R\) when \(\theta = 60^\circ\) and \(\theta = 30^\circ\) at the same speed?

    Both give the same range, because \(\sin 120^\circ = \sin 60^\circ\).

    Chapter 2

    Rotational Motion

    2.1 Introduction to rotational motion

    Definition

    Rotational motion is the motion of an object around a fixed axis, in which its particles move along circular paths centred on that axis.

    Examples:

    • A rotating fan.
    • A spinning wheel.
    • A rotating gear.
    • A clock's hands.

    2.1.1 Classification of rotational motion

    Rotational motion can be classified according to whether angular velocity remains constant or changes.

    • Uniform rotational motion: angular velocity remains constant.
    • Non-uniform rotational motion: angular velocity changes with time.

    To understand these types, we first need three fundamental quantities: angular displacement, angular velocity and angular acceleration.

    2.2 Angular displacement

    2.2.1 Definition

    Definition

    Angular displacement is the angle through which an object rotates about a fixed axis, measured from its initial angular position to its final angular position.

    Symbol and SI unit
    • \(\theta\): angular displacement
    • SI unit: radian (rad)

    2.2.2 Derivation of angular displacement formula

    Derivation

    Consider a circle of radius \(r\), where an object moves along an arc of length \(s\). By the definition of the radian:

    \[\theta = \frac{\text{Arc length}}{\text{Radius}}\]
    Angular displacement \[\boxed{\theta = \frac{s}{r}}\]

    Rearranging:

    Arc length \[\boxed{s = r\theta}\]
    Important

    This relationship requires angular displacement to be measured in radians.

    2.2.3 Angular measurement and conversion

    A complete revolution represents one complete circle. The circumference of a circle is \(C = 2\pi r\). Using \(\theta = s/r\), for one complete revolution \(\theta = \dfrac{2\pi r}{r}\):

    Conversion \[\boxed{1\ \text{revolution} = 2\pi\ \text{rad} = 360^\circ}\] \[180^\circ = \pi\ \text{rad}\]
    Degree to radian \[\boxed{\theta_{rad} = \theta_{deg}\cdot\frac{\pi}{180}}\]
    Radian to degree \[\boxed{\theta_{deg} = \theta_{rad}\cdot\frac{180}{\pi}}\]

    2.2.4 Worked example

    Worked example 2.1

    A rotating wheel has a radius of 0.5 m. A point on its edge travels an arc length of 2 m. Calculate the angular displacement.

    Given: \(s = 2\ \text{m},\quad r = 0.5\ \text{m}\)

    Required: \(\theta\)

    1. Angular displacement. Formula: \(\theta = \dfrac{s}{r}\).
      Substitution: \(\theta = \dfrac{2}{0.5}\)
      \(\theta = 4\ \text{rad}\)

    2.3 Angular velocity

    2.3.1 Definition

    Definition

    Angular velocity is the rate of change of angular displacement with respect to time. It describes how quickly an object rotates and in which rotational direction.

    Symbol and SI unit
    • \(\omega\): angular velocity
    • SI unit: rad/s

    2.3.2 Derivation of angular velocity formula

    Derivation

    Average linear velocity is displacement divided by time. Similarly, average angular velocity is angular displacement divided by the time interval:

    \[\boxed{\omega_{avg} = \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}}\]

    For constant angular velocity:

    \[\boxed{\omega = \frac{\theta}{t}}\]

    2.3.3 Relationship between angular velocity, frequency and period

    Symbols and SI units
    • \(f\): frequency, the number of complete revolutions per second (Hz)
    • \(T\): period, the time for one revolution (s)
    Derivation

    One revolution corresponds to \(\theta = 2\pi\) radians. Using \(\omega = \theta/t\), for one complete revolution taking time \(T\):

    Angular velocity and period \[\boxed{\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}}\]

    Since frequency is the reciprocal of period:

    Frequency and period \[\boxed{f = \frac{1}{T}}\]

    Substituting:

    Angular velocity and frequency \[\boxed{\omega = 2\pi f}\]

    2.3.4 Worked example

    Worked example 2.2

    A rotating wheel completes 120 revolutions in 60 seconds. Calculate its frequency, period and angular velocity.

    Given: \(N = 120\ \text{revolutions},\quad t = 60\ \text{s}\)

    1. Frequency. Formula: \(f = \dfrac{\text{Number of revolutions}}{\text{Time}}\).
      Substitution: \(f = \dfrac{120}{60}\)
      \(f = 2\ \text{Hz}\)
    2. Period. Formula: \(T = \dfrac{1}{f}\).
      Substitution: \(T = \dfrac{1}{2}\)
      \(T = 0.5\ \text{s}\)
    3. Angular velocity. Formula: \(\omega = 2\pi f\).
      Substitution: \(\omega = 2\pi(2)\)
      \(\omega = 4\pi \approx 12.57\ \text{rad/s}\)

    2.4 Angular acceleration

    2.4.1 Definition

    Definition

    Angular acceleration is the rate of change of angular velocity with respect to time. It describes how quickly the rotational speed changes.

    Symbol and SI unit
    • \(\alpha\): angular acceleration
    • SI unit: rad/s²

    2.4.2 Derivation of angular acceleration formula

    Derivation

    Average linear acceleration is \(a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}\). Similarly, average angular acceleration is:

    \[\alpha = \frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta t}\]
    Angular acceleration \[\boxed{\alpha = \frac{\omega_f - \omega_i}{t}}\]
    • \(\omega_f\): final angular velocity (rad/s)
    • \(\omega_i\): initial angular velocity (rad/s)
    • \(t\): elapsed time (s)

    2.4.3 Worked example

    Worked example 2.3

    A wheel initially rotates at 5 rad/s. Its angular velocity increases to 25 rad/s in 4 seconds. Calculate the angular acceleration.

    Given: \(\omega_i = 5\ \text{rad/s},\quad \omega_f = 25\ \text{rad/s},\quad t = 4\ \text{s}\)

    1. Angular acceleration. Formula: \(\alpha = \dfrac{\omega_f - \omega_i}{t}\).
      Substitution: \(\alpha = \dfrac{25 - 5}{4} = \dfrac{20}{4}\)
      \(\alpha = 5\ \text{rad/s}^2\)

    2.5 Equations of rotational motion

    For constant angular acceleration, rotational motion has equations corresponding to the equations of linear motion. This connection comes from the same kinematic principles: constant acceleration.

    Linear motionRotational motion
    \(v = u + at\)\(\omega_f = \omega_i + \alpha t\)
    \(s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2\)\(\theta = \omega_i t + \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^2\)
    \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\)\(\omega_f^2 = \omega_i^2 + 2\alpha\theta\)

    Worked example 2.4

    Worked example 2.4

    A wheel starts from rest and rotates with a constant angular acceleration of \(3\ \text{rad/s}^2\) for 4 seconds. Find its final angular velocity and angular displacement.

    Given: \(\omega_i = 0,\quad \alpha = 3\ \text{rad/s}^2,\quad t = 4\ \text{s}\)

    1. Final angular velocity. Formula: \(\omega_f = \omega_i + \alpha t\).
      Substitution: \(\omega_f = 0 + 3(4)\)
      \(\omega_f = 12\ \text{rad/s}\)
    2. Angular displacement. Formula: \(\theta = \omega_i t + \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^2\).
      Substitution: \(\theta = 0(4) + \tfrac{1}{2}(3)(4^2) = 1.5(16)\)
      \(\theta = 24\ \text{rad}\)

    2.6 Relationship between angular and linear quantities

    A rotating object has angular motion around its axis, while points on the object also travel along circular paths. This allows us to connect rotational quantities with linear quantities.

    2.6.1 Linear velocity and angular velocity

    Derivation

    We already derived \(s = r\theta\). Divide both sides by time:

    \[\frac{s}{t} = r\,\frac{\theta}{t}\]

    Since \(v = \dfrac{s}{t}\) and \(\omega = \dfrac{\theta}{t}\), we obtain:

    Linear and angular velocity \[\boxed{v = r\omega}\]

    2.6.2 Tangential acceleration and angular acceleration

    Derivation

    Starting with \(v = r\omega\), differentiate with respect to time for a fixed radius:

    \[\frac{dv}{dt} = r\,\frac{d\omega}{dt}\]

    Since \(a_t = \dfrac{dv}{dt}\) and \(\alpha = \dfrac{d\omega}{dt}\):

    Tangential acceleration \[\boxed{a_t = r\alpha}\]

    2.6.3 Centripetal acceleration

    Even when an object moves at constant speed around a circle, its velocity changes because its direction changes. A change in velocity means acceleration exists. This inward acceleration is called centripetal acceleration.

    Centripetal acceleration \[\boxed{a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}}\]

    Using \(v = r\omega\):

    \[a_c = \frac{(r\omega)^2}{r} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{a_c = r\omega^2}\]
    Symbols and SI units
    • \(v\): linear (tangential) velocity (m/s)
    • \(r\): radius (m)
    • \(a_t\): tangential acceleration (m/s²)
    • \(a_c\): centripetal acceleration (m/s²)

    2.6.4 Worked example

    Worked example 2.5

    A wheel of radius 0.4 m rotates with an angular velocity of 10 rad/s and an angular acceleration of 2 rad/s². Find:

    • Linear velocity.
    • Tangential acceleration.
    • Centripetal acceleration.

    Given: \(r = 0.4\ \text{m},\quad \omega = 10\ \text{rad/s},\quad \alpha = 2\ \text{rad/s}^2\)

    1. Linear velocity. Formula: \(v = r\omega\).
      Substitution: \(v = 0.4(10)\)
      \(v = 4\ \text{m/s}\)
    2. Tangential acceleration. Formula: \(a_t = r\alpha\).
      Substitution: \(a_t = 0.4(2)\)
      \(a_t = 0.8\ \text{m/s}^2\)
    3. Centripetal acceleration. Formula: \(a_c = r\omega^2\).
      Substitution: \(a_c = 0.4(10)^2\)
      \(a_c = 40\ \text{m/s}^2\)

    2.7 Centripetal force

    2.7.1 Definition

    Definition

    Centripetal force is the resultant inward force required to keep an object moving along a circular path.

    2.7.2 Derivation

    Derivation

    Using Newton's second law \(F = ma\). For circular motion, the inward acceleration is centripetal acceleration, so \(F_c = m\,a_c\):

    Centripetal force \[\boxed{F_c = \frac{m v^2}{r}}\]

    Since \(v = r\omega\), this can also be written as \(F_c = m r\omega^2\).

    Chapter 2: Unit summary

    Summary
    • Rotational motion is motion about a fixed axis; uniform means constant angular velocity.
    • Angular displacement \(\theta = s/r\) is measured in radians; \(2\pi\ \text{rad} = 360^\circ\).
    • Angular velocity \(\omega = \theta/t = 2\pi/T = 2\pi f\), with \(f = 1/T\).
    • Angular acceleration \(\alpha = (\omega_f - \omega_i)/t\).
    • For constant \(\alpha\): \(\omega_f = \omega_i + \alpha t\), \(\theta = \omega_i t + \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^2\), \(\omega_f^2 = \omega_i^2 + 2\alpha\theta\).
    • Linear–angular links: \(v = r\omega\), \(a_t = r\alpha\), \(a_c = v^2/r = r\omega^2\).
    • Centripetal force \(F_c = mv^2/r = mr\omega^2\).

    Chapter 2: Exam-focused points

    Exam focus
    • Convert all angles to radians before using \(s = r\theta\) or \(\omega = \theta/t\).
    • Remember \(f = 1/T\) and \(\omega = 2\pi f\); many questions give revolutions and time, so compute \(f\) first.
    • Use the rotational kinematic equations only when angular acceleration is constant.
    • Distinguish linear quantities (\(v, a_t\)) from angular quantities (\(\omega, \alpha\)); the link is always through \(r\).
    • Centripetal acceleration acts inward even at constant speed, because direction changes.
    • Show the substitution with units in every step to avoid losing marks.
    Self-check: a wheel of radius 0.5 m turns through 4 rad. What arc length does a point on the rim travel?

    Self-check: why is \(a_c\) nonzero for uniform circular motion?

    Because the direction of velocity changes continuously, so there is an inward acceleration even when the speed is constant.
